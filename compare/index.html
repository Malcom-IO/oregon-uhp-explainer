How the five revenue models compare (unofficial)

The Governance Board drafted five ways to raise the money for the plan, each mixing a household contribution and an employer payroll tax. Here they are side by side: what each would raise, what a household like yours would pay, and who pays across incomes.

This page is unofficial. It is not published, reviewed or endorsed by the Universal Health Plan Governance Board or the State of Oregon. It is based on the Board's October 2026 draft documents and a later legislative brief, as listed in SOURCES.md. Corrections go to the project's issues.

Who pays, by income

New taxes as a share of each group's income. As the Board does, Oregonians are ranked by the income of the tax return they are on and split into fifths of people, with the more than 600,000 residents on no Oregon return in the lowest fifth. The top fifth is also broken down.

  • Household contribution
  • Employer payroll tax, counted against workers' pay

As the Board does, the employer payroll tax is counted against workers' pay: employers are assumed to pay it out of wages. That is why groups that owe no household contribution still show a share. Each model's 2022 payroll tax is spread over the IRS table's $114.4 billion of resident wages (our choice; the Board's own base is about $107 billion, which would put every fifth about half a point above the Board's published rates). The corporate taxes are not shown here. Built from the IRS's 2022 Oregon returns, the year the Board's rates were set on.

How this is calculated

What you would pay uses the calculator's own rules and figures from the Board's draft report and Appendix FR.

What each model raises is the Legislative Revenue Office's preliminary 2032 projection, from the revised legislative brief dated October 22, 2026. It is later than the October 2026 drafts the rest of this project uses.

Who pays applies the same household rules to every Oregon tax return in 2022, the year the Board's rates were set on. The IRS publishes returns by income bracket, not one by one, so we spread each bracket's returns across it (our method). The IRS table has no income for residents on no return, so they are counted as people with no income, only to place the fifths.

Checked against the Board. The project's tests compare these figures with the Board's published ones for every model:

The lowest fifth comes out a little higher than the Board's, and the share owing nothing lower: the Board's return file likely holds more low-income returns than the IRS state tables. The income groups' starting points, such as about in 2026 dollars for the top 1%, are our estimates from the same spread of returns. Within the IRS's open "$1 million or more" bracket, the split between the top 0.1% and the rest of the top 1% is an estimate too.
