How we know these numbers

Household contribution. Built return by return from the IRS's 2022 Oregon statistics (2.03 million returns by income bracket), spread within each bracket, using income less taxable Social Security as the Board does. The rates are set on 2022 incomes and grown to 2032 by the Legislative Revenue Office's projections for Models 2–5 (×). Brackets are shown in 2026 dollars and double for returns with two or more people, as in the Board's appendix. The "richest 1%" and "top 20%" extras apply per return and are not doubled.

Employer payroll tax. No tax on a private employer's first $500,000; one rate to $10 million and another above. Public employers pay the top rate from the first dollar. The payroll bases are the ones the Board's own 2022 results imply, grown × to 2032. The worker credit gives back a share of the payroll tax paid on your wages.

Other taxes. Sales tax: the Legislative Revenue Office's estimate that 5% raises $5.85B in 2026–27, grown 4.4% a year. Property: Oregon's $532.5B of net assessed value (FY 2024–25), grown 3.8% a year; a new statewide levy would need a constitutional change (Measure 5). Corporate income tax: $0.27B per point in 2032; corporate activity tax: 0.113% raises $0.39B (both LRO). Cigarettes: $2 a pack raises about $150M a year (LRO, 2020).

Who bears what. Payroll and wage taxes fall on workers in proportion to wages, as in the Board's tables. Sales, activity, cigarette and property taxes fall in proportion to spending, which rises more slowly than income (BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey, 2025). Corporate income taxes fall 75% on owners of capital and 25% on workers.

Behavior. These are static estimates, like the Board's. High rates on a small group invite avoidance and moves out of state, which the Board's committee discussed.

The Board's rates were set on 2022 incomes. Carried to 2032, the LRO projects $32.4B to $34.8B, above the Board's $30B estimate, so final rates would be recalibrated. The "owes nothing" share comes out about 8 points lower than the Board's in every model; the Board's return counts likely include filers the IRS state tables leave out. The LRO's Model 1 figure uses a different credit for Medicare Part B premiums.
